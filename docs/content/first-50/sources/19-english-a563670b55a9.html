Winter Melon and Pork Rib Soup (Dong Gua Pai Gu Tang)

August in Guangzhou is a wet oven. The air hangs thick with humidity, and nobody has much appetite by dinner. This is the soup my grandmother pulled out of the heat season: a bowl of clear broth, pale green winter melon floating like translucent jade, and ribs simmered to a sweet, giving tenderness. One bowl, and the weight of the day lifts a little.

There is nothing dramatic about it — no sizzle, no lacquered sauce, no showy garnish. That is exactly the point. Dong gua pai gu tang is the quietest member of the Cantonese soup canon, and the one I cook most often. Two ingredients do all the talking: a chunk of pale green winter melon and a handful of pork ribs, with nothing but water, ginger, scallion, and time.

The best bowls I have eaten come out of clay pots at my aunt’s table, cloudy with collagen and barely seasoned. Mine never tastes like hers — and she tells me hers never matched her own mother’s either. That is how family soups work. What I can share is the method behind that silky, faintly grassy broth, and why each step matters.

What Is Winter Melon and Pork Rib Soup (Dong Gua Pai Gu Tang)?

Winter melon and pork rib soup — dong gua pai gu tang (冬瓜排骨汤) — is a classic Cantonese clear soup: pork ribs simmered with thick slices of winter melon in plain water until the broth turns silky and sweet. Winter melon is a pale green gourd whose flesh is more than 95 percent water — prized in southern China for centuries as a cooling summer food. In the summer months, Cantonese households trade rich, dark soups for clear ones, and dong gua pai gu tang tops the list — one of a whole family of rib-and-vegetable broths that includes my homestyle lotus root and pork rib soup.

In Chinese cooking, soup is as much about well-being as flavor. Winter melon is traditionally counted among the “cooling” foods, recommended when the body feels overheated — one reason Cantonese cooks rely on it all summer. The ribs contribute a mild sweetness that anchors the melon’s faint grassiness. Order it in Guangzhou and Hong Kong year-round, but it truly comes into its own from June through September — served warm, oddly comforting, and deeply refreshing.

If you love a clean, collagen-rich bowl, you will like my homestyle clear oxtail soup — the same slow-simmered clarity, built on beefy oxtail instead of ribs.

Why This Recipe Works

  • A bare-bones list that lets the pork speak: no soy, no stock cubes, no sugar — just ribs, water, ginger, and scallion, so the broth stays clear and pork-sweet.
  • Blanching first guarantees a clean pot: a quick boil and rinse removes the blood proteins that would otherwise cloud the broth and turn it bitter.
  • Low, steady simmering extracts collagen slowly: an hour at a gentle bubble dissolves the ribs’ connective tissue into gelatin, giving the soup its silky body.
  • Winter melon goes in last, on purpose: added for the final 15 minutes, it softens to a melting translucency without dissolving.
  • Cold water to start, no topping up: starting cold and simmering without adding water keeps every bit of collagen you build, so the broth is concentrated and clean.

Equipment Needed

  • A clay pot (sand pot) with a lid — or any heavy-bottomed pot
  • A large pot for blanching the ribs
  • A slotted spoon for lifting and skimming
  • A chef’s knife and a cutting board
  • A fine mesh strainer or ladle for the final skim

Ingredients

  • 500 g (1.1 lb) pork ribs
  • 600 g (1.3 lb) winter melon, sliced about 1/4 in (6 mm) thick
  • 1 scallion, tied in a knot
  • 1 small piece of fresh ginger, sliced
  • Salt, to taste
  • Sliced scallions, to finish

Ingredient Notes

Pork ribs. Meaty spareribs are the classic choice; baby back ribs work too. Ask the butcher for ribs with generous meat and little hard fat — the meat sweetens the broth while the collagen gives it body. Leave them whole or cut into single-bone lengths; no need to remove the membrane on the back, the simmer tenderizes it.

Winter melon. Look for one with a waxy white bloom on the skin and dense, firm flesh. Only the pale flesh is cooked here; rind and seeds are discarded. If you have never used it, think of it as a squash that tastes of almost nothing and absorbs everything around it — the same idea that makes my steamed winter melon with minced pork so gentle.

Why the clay pot. A clay pot (sha guo) heats slowly and holds heat evenly, keeping the soup at a gentle, constant simmer instead of surging and subsiding like thin metal pots. That steady heat is what a clear broth wants. No clay pot? A stainless pot or Dutch oven works fine — just watch the heat more carefully at the start.

Prep & Mise en Place

This soup is barely any work, but the few minutes of prep matter. Tie the scallion in a knot so it stays whole through the long simmer, and slice the ginger into thin coins; no need to peel either. Then tackle the melon: slice off the rind, scoop out the seeds, and cut the flesh into even pieces about 1/4 inch (6 mm) thick so they cook at the same rate. Keep the melon aside — it goes in near the end. Fill a second pot of water for blanching and set a bowl of cold water ready.

Cooking Process

Step 1 — Tie the Scallion, Slice the Ginger and Winter Melon

Tie the scallion in a knot, slice the ginger, and slice the winter melon about 1/4 inch (6 mm) thick. Set them aside; they will sit in the pot for the full hour.

Step 2 — Blanch the Ribs

Wash the ribs under cold running water, then boil them in a pot of boiling water for 2 to 3 minutes, until the surface turns pale and gray foam rises. Drain, rinse in fresh water, and skim off any remaining foam.

Blanching is the difference between a broth that glows and one that tastes murky. Blood and marrow proteins in raw meat denature and coagulate in boiling water into the gray scum that clouds a soup and muddies its flavor. Removing that scum now — a boil, a rinse, a skim — means the long simmer produces a clear, sweet, clean-tasting broth.

Step 3 — Simmer Slowly in a Clay Pot

Put cold water in the clay pot — about 6 cups (1.4 L) — and add the blanched ribs, scallion knot, and ginger. Bring to a boil over high heat, then reduce to low and barely simmer, covered, for about 1 hour. Do not add water during the simmer. Add the winter melon and cook about 15 minutes more, until the slices turn translucent and yield to a chopstick. Season with salt, scatter sliced scallions, and serve hot.

Slow simmering is collagen’s favorite mood. When the pot barely bubbles for an hour, the collagen in the ribs’ connective tissue slowly unwinds into gelatin — the protein that gives the soup its silky body. A hard rolling boil does the opposite: it churns rendered fat into the water and turns the broth milky. Winter melon, meanwhile, is over 95 percent water; as it cooks, its cell walls release water into the soup, which is why the slices turn translucent and the broth gains a gentle sweetness and a slippery texture Cantonese cooks call smooth on the throat.

Common Mistakes When Making Winter Melon and Pork Rib Soup

1. The broth comes out cloudy or bitter. Cause: the ribs were not blanched. Fix: boil them 2 to 3 minutes, rinse, and skim the foam — that scum causes both the cloud and the off-flavor.

2. The soup tastes thin. Cause: too much water, or water added mid-simmer. Fix: use just enough cold water to cover the ribs generously (about 6 cups for 500 g), and never top up — the gentle reduction concentrates the gelatin.

3. The broth is greasy. Cause: the heat crept up and the soup boiled hard, emulsifying the rendered fat. Fix: keep the pot at a bare simmer — a few lazy bubbles, never a churning boil. If fat still sits on top, skim it after the winter melon goes in.

4. The winter melon disappears into mush. Cause: it went in with the ribs and cooked for the whole hour. Fix: add it only for the final 15 minutes; the pieces should be tender enough to pierce but still hold their shape.

5. The soup is salty one day and bland the next. Cause: salting at the start, so the salt concentrates as the pot reduces. Fix: season only at the end, tasting as you go — lightly seasoned, since the melon and ribs keep giving up sweetness.

6. The ribs are tough and dry. Cause: high heat or too little time. Fix: an honest hour at a low simmer; if the ribs still resist the chopstick, give them another 15 to 20 minutes — low and slow is the whole recipe.

Variations

This is the blank canvas of Cantonese clear soups, and every family seasons it differently. For more sweetness, swap some of the melon for corn and daikon in this pork rib soup with corn and daikon — the corn adds a honeyed note that pairs beautifully. Add dried goji berries in the last 10 minutes for color, or drop in two or three dried shiitake for a woodsier broth. Red dates (hong zao) give a subtly sweet slant. Swap the ribs for pork trotters for a silkier, gelatin-rich result, or keep it vegetarian with dried mushrooms and tofu.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does winter melon taste like? Very little on its own — faintly sweet, grassy, mild, closer to a soft cucumber than a pumpkin. Its job is texture: it soaks up the pork broth and turns jelly-soft, while its own water sweetens the soup.

Can I use a regular pot instead of a clay pot? Yes. A stainless pot or Dutch oven works well; bring it to the boil more gently and check the heat after the first 10 minutes, since metal conducts more aggressively than clay.

Why can’t I add water during the simmer? Topping up dilutes the collagen you have spent the hour building. If the level looks low, reduce the heat slightly — a generous start of water keeps the meat just covered.

Why is my soup cloudy? Almost always because the ribs were not blanched or the pot boiled too hard. Skim the blanching scum, keep the simmer lazy, and the broth stays clear. Skim a spoonful of fat near the end if needed.

Can I make it ahead? Absolutely. Refrigerate for up to 3 days, skimming solidified fat before reheating. The winter melon softens further, so cook it toward the tender side if you plan to store the soup.

How to Serve

Serve the soup as the center of a Cantonese family meal: a bowl of steamed rice, this pot in the middle, and a couple of quick dishes around it. It arrives first, warm rather than scalding. It pairs naturally with other pork dishes like easy braised pork ribs with rice or the gentler easy homestyle steamed pork ribs. On a lighter night, add a second soup — my easy tofu mushroom soup for a vegetarian counterpoint, or an easy homestyle hot and sour soup for vinegar heat. Leftover melon and broth are wonderful poured over rice the next morning.

Storage & Reheat

Cool the soup to room temperature, then refrigerate in a covered container for up to 3 days; the flavors deepen overnight. Lift off any solidified fat before reheating gently on the stovetop or in the microwave until steaming hot — at least 165°F (74°C) — rather than boiling hard, which turns the melon to mush. It freezes well for up to 2 months, though the melon softens further. Per USDA guidance, pork is safe once it reaches 145°F (63°C) with a rest — the long simmer far exceeds that — and leftovers should be refrigerated within 2 hours of cooking.
